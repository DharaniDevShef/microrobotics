This project investigates whether evolutionary search can be used to design modular robots whose shape, folding behaviour and locomotion are considered together. The chosen platform is the Roblet system, in which small modules join through magnetic connectors, fold from a flat arrangement into a three-dimensional structure, and move under an externally applied magnetic field.

The work developed a graph-based representation of Roblet morphologies and a grammar that limits changes to physically meaningful configurations. Candidate designs were mirrored, checked for collisions and evaluated in MuJoCo. Four objectives were used: folded-gait velocity, change in shape entropy during folding, yaw response to a light stimulus, and speed response to a light stimulus. Attractive and repulsive light responses were treated as separate runs because they require opposite optimisation directions. NSGA-III was used to preserve trade-offs between these objectives, while a proximal policy optimisation (PPO) actor-critic selected grammar actions for mutation and crossover.

The available experiments used a population of 30 individuals, 200 generations, 60-second rollouts and random seed 42. \imageinference{The convergence and hypervolume figures indicate measurable progress during the reported run; the exact values and any plateau should be confirmed from the plotted data before submission.} The small comparison run also showed that the random baseline achieved a higher best scalarised fitness than the RL-assisted run at this scale. Overall, the project establishes a working physics-in-the-loop framework for studying automated morphology design on a self-assembling robotic platform.

\subsection{Automated and Physics-Aware Robot Co-Design}

Imagine asking a robot to design its own body. The fundamental challenge is not simply training a neural network policy to make a fixed physical platform walk; it is discovering the machine's geometric shape, joint distribution, and dynamic behavior simultaneously. A morphology optimized purely for forward locomotion speed often exhibits poor physical stability. Similarly, a modular body that folds elegantly may fail to transmit magnetic torque effectively, while a configuration that maximizes light-sensor sensitivity might sacrifice movement velocity. In this setting, physical morphology is not a passive container for intelligence, it is an active component of the computational process.

Traditional robotic engineering strictly decouples these design choices into a sequential pipeline. An engineer first specifies the geometric topology, a mechanical modeler tunes joint kinematics, and a control engineer subsequently searches for a control policy that makes the completed platform behave correctly. While effective for highly structured environments, this manual approach restricts exploration to human intuition, often filtering out non-intuitive, high-performing structures before they are ever evaluated in simulation. Automated robot co-design reverses this pipeline: candidate morphologies are generated algorithmically, instantiated in a physics engine, evaluated against task-specific fitness metrics, and returned to the search loop. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:automated-design-hook}, this paradigm merges evolutionary algorithms for macro-level structural search with reinforcement learning to evaluate or guide architectural edits.

This computational playbook is reflected across key milestones in modern robotics. Large-scale GPU environments such as NVIDIA Isaac Sim (Figure~\ref{fig:isaac-sim}) demonstrate how evaluating thousands of parallel physics instances drastically accelerates policy learning. In physical hardware, platforms like Boston Dynamics' Atlas (Figure~\ref{fig:boston-dynamics}) highlight how structural dynamics and high-bandwidth control must operate in tight coupling for agile, highly dynamic movement. Crucially, benchmarks like Evolution Gym (Figure~\ref{fig:evolution-gym}) establish formal abstractions for morphology-control co-design, decoupling structural generation from behavioral evaluation \citep{bhatia2021evolution,li2023modular,yang2024machine}.

The challenge becomes significantly sharper when applied to modular, self-assembling systems. Unlike fixed quadruped or humanoid frames, modular units can combine into an exponential number of topological graphs. For planar systems like Roblets, each graph topology directly determines how 2D flat sheets transform into 3D folded modules (Figure~\ref{fig:self-folding}), how folded contacts interact with ground friction, and how the physical body responds to external magnetic fields or light signals. Furthermore, local mechanical interlocks and light responses offer a direct pathway toward collective swarm intelligence.

The objective of this dissertation is to bridge these paradigms: uniting evolutionary multi-objective search, deep reinforcement learning structural edit policies, and physics-in-the-loop evaluation inside a modular pipeline where a candidate design begins as an abstract graph, self-folds into a 3D physical body, and is evaluated directly by its embodied physical capabilities.

\subsection{The Roblet Platform}
% [~0.6 page]
% Brief here (1 paragraph) - full detail belongs in Chapter 3. Just

Roblets are small, modular units with magnetic bonding sites and foldable sections. The units can be assembled from a two-dimensional arrangement and then folded into a three-dimensional robot. Locomotion is produced by an externally applied oscillating magnetic field rather than by motors placed in every module. This combination makes Roblets a useful test case for automated design because morphology affects assembly, folding, magnetic response and motion at the same time \citep{han2023roblets}.

\subsection{Swarm Communication via Light-Based Virtual Pheromones}
% [~0.7 page]
% Motivate why light-triggered reactive folding is interesting as a
% PRIMITIVE for swarm behaviour (turn toward/away, speed up/slow down in
% response to a neighbour's signal) even though this project evolves and
% tests these primitives on a SINGLE robot, not a multi-robot swarm -
% be explicit about that scope limitation here (don't let a reader
% assume swarm experiments were run - see also Chapter 7 Limitations).
Light-sensitive joints provide a simple local reaction primitive. A joint can respond to a one-sided light stimulus by changing the robot's turning behaviour, or to a wider stimulus by changing its speed. These responses are relevant to virtual pheromone communication because a robot could, in principle, move towards or away from a signal produced by another robot. In this project the primitive was evaluated on one simulated robot at a time. No multi-robot swarm experiment was carried out, so the results concern a building block of swarm behaviour rather than swarm coordination itself.

Existing Roblet morphologies are designed by hand, which limits the range of structures that can be considered \citep{han2023roblets}. The difficulty is increased because a candidate is defined by interacting module types, port connections, module count, fold angles and light-response settings. Even before all graph connections are counted, allowing between 2 and 40 modules creates a large discrete search space.

The problem addressed in this report is the limited integration of morphology generation, self-folding behaviour and locomotion performance in an evolutionary robotic system. The central hypothesis is that an ML-assisted evolutionary framework can generate legal two-dimensional Roblet configurations, evaluate their folded three-dimensional behaviour and use the resulting measurements to improve the population. A second question is whether entropy-based folding complexity and light-response measures can reveal relationships between morphology and behaviour. The random-baseline experiment is used later to assess the selected ML mechanism; it is not the main hypothesis.

Modular robotic systems build functional machines by combining standardized physical building blocks. The foundational principles of programmable self-assembly established by Klavins (2007) demonstrated that complex physical macro-structures could emerge from simple units governed by local stochastic interactions and discrete bonding rules \citep{4272328}. Early modular architectures, however, relied primarily on rigid mechanical latches and required structured, high-energy environments to successfully assemble.

Over the last two decades, hardware platforms have progressed significantly toward parallelized self-assembly and higher operational autonomy. Systems such as SMORES-EP (Liu et al., 2023) achieve versatile reconfiguration by leveraging electro-permanent magnets, enabling low-power dynamic coupling \citep{liu2023smores}. Similarly, recent advancements in spherical freeform modular robots (Tu et al., 2026) demonstrate high degrees of self-reconfiguration autonomy and flexible locomotion across unstructured terrain \citep{tu2026locomotion}. Taking the concept of physical adaptation further, Wyder et al. (2025) proposed the concept of "robot metabolism," where machines dynamically adapt by consuming and reallocating structural components from other modular units \citep{wyder2025robot}. While these platforms illustrate significant mechanical flexibility, comprehensive surveys of the field (Alattas et al., 2019) highlight a recurring bottleneck: as the number of available physical interfaces scales, hand-crafting effective topologies quickly becomes intractable due to the massive combinatorial search space \citep{alattas2019evolutionary}.

The Roblet platform (Han et al., 2023) represents a distinct paradigm within modular robotics. A Roblet begins as a flat, 2D 3D-printed unit equipped with magnetic "jigsaw smart-glue" passive bonding interfaces. When exposed to thermal activation (e.g., hot water), heat-responsive PVC hinges trigger precise self-folding mechanisms that transform the 2D sheet into a 3D functional module. Once folded, locomotion is driven externally through oscillating magnetic fields, while light-based "virtual pheromones" govern collective swarm behavior without requiring bulky onboard motors or battery packs. 

However, despite these innovative mechanics, existing Roblet configurations reported in prior art (Han et al., 2023) remain strictly hand-designed. Human intuition struggles to predict how complex 2D planar layouts will fold into 3D multi-hinge structures, or how those 3D shapes will interact with oscillating magnetic fields to produce stable gaits. To unlock the potential of the Roblet platform, an automated, computational co-design approach is required.

Real-world robot design inherently involves balancing conflicting performance requirements. A morphology optimized purely for forward locomotion speed often exhibits poor stability or excessive structural complexity. Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithms (MOEAs) address this by discovering a Pareto front, a set of non-dominated solutions representing optimal trade-offs between competing criteria.

Standard multi-objective algorithms, such as NSGA-II, rely on crowding distance metrics to maintain population diversity along the Pareto front. However, when the optimization problem involves more than three objectives (many-objective optimization), crowding distance metrics lose selectivity because almost all individuals become non-dominated relative to one another. To resolve this, Deb and Jain (2014) developed NSGA-III, which replaces crowding-distance sorting with reference-direction-based niching. By projecting candidate solutions onto uniformly distributed reference lines in objective space, NSGA-III maintains high population diversity and convergence pressure across higher-dimensional trade-off surfaces.

In robotic co-design, integrating machine learning with MOEAs is relevant when the design space is constrained. Ming et al. (2024) provides a related example of deep reinforcement learning selecting genetic operators in constrained multi-objective optimization \citep{ming2024constrained}. This project applies NSGA-III to a four-objective optimization landscape:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Maximize folded-gait locomotion velocity ($f_1$),
    \item Maximize entropy-based folding complexity gain ($\Delta H = H_{\text{3D}} - H_{\text{2D}}$) ($f_2$),
    \item Maximize/minimize light-based pheromone yaw response ($f_3$),
    \item Maximize/minimize light-based pheromone speed response ($f_4$).
\end{enumerate}
NSGA-III is well-justified for this many-objective setting because its reference-direction niching is designed to maintain diversity and selection pressure when several objectives make ordinary crowding-based selection less discriminating \citep{fan2014evolutionary}.

\section{Actuation and Self-Folding Principles}
% [~1 page] MARKING: Report Quality only (LOW-MED), same reason as 3.1.
% Mechanism detail: heat-responsive PVC hinges (2D->3D self-folding),
% external oscillating magnetic field for locomotion (magnetic torque
% on embedded magnets), light-based virtual pheromone signalling as the
% swarm-communication primitive this project's pheromone objectives are
% built on (forward-reference Chapter 4 Section 4.4 here).
The physical design uses heat-responsive PVC hinges to convert the flat arrangement into a folded structure. In the simulation, this process is represented by allowing connected module bodies to settle into their folded configuration rather than prescribing the final pose directly. The assembled robot is then driven by an oscillating magnetic field. Each embedded magnet experiences a torque that depends on the field direction, and the combined torques produce the motion of the whole assembly.

The light-response mechanism is represented as a change at selected foldable joints. A one-sided stimulus is used to measure yaw response, while a wider stimulus is used to measure speed response. These two measurements form the reaction primitives used later in the evolutionary objective set. The simulator therefore provides a common route from morphology to folding, actuation and measurable behaviour.

\subsection{Bilateral Symmetry and Mirror Construction}

Only one half of the morphology is evolved directly. The root module and its spine child provide the mirror anchors, while the expandable side is connected through the remaining port. The representation reduces redundant solutions without removing the structural edits available to the evolutionary operators.

The operator set contains eight mutations and two crossovers: add node, delete node, prune subtree, change fold type, change hinge angle, reconnect a port, toggle a light sensor, change the light hinge angle, graft a subtree and swap subtrees. Each action is masked according to the current graph. Grammar masking was selected instead of generating an illegal graph and rejecting it afterwards. Reject-and-retry wastes simulation and policy samples, while a soft penalty asks the policy to learn rules that can be enforced directly. \subsection{Attractive versus Repulsive Response Configuration}
% [~0.5 page] MARKING: Understanding and Analysis (MED-HIGH) - continues
% the AO-1 methodology evidence started in 4.2.2.
% Explain why attractive/repulsive are two SEPARATE evolutionary runs

Attractive and repulsive behaviour were implemented as separate evolutionary runs. Both runs use the same signed measurements: positive yaw denotes turning towards the light and positive speed response denotes speeding up. The optimisation direction is then changed for the repulsive run so that turning away and slowing down are preferred. A single run containing both directions would ask the same light-hinge variable to increase and decrease at once, creating a self-contradictory objective pair. Separate runs make the intended behaviour explicit and allow their morphologies to be compared directly.

The structural-edit policy receives an ordered pair of parent half-genotypes. Each graph is represented by nine node features: module type, normalised hinge angle, depth, free-port fraction, root status, light sensitivity and light-hinge angle. A Graph Transformer encoder produces representations for the variable-size graphs, using hidden dimension 32, two Transformer blocks and four attention heads. Separate actor and critic networks use this graph representation and a global mean readout.

The action is sampled hierarchically. First, the actor selects one of eight mutation actions or two crossover actions. It then selects legal target nodes, ports and parameters. The mask is applied at each stage, so the policy cannot select a root deletion, an unavailable port or an action that exceeds the module limit. The reward is the change in scalarised four-objective fitness from parent to child, with a penalty for an unstable or failed simulation. PPO is updated once per generation using the offspring decisions as one-step transitions.

This policy guides variation, not locomotion control. The policy chooses how the graph is changed, while MuJoCo supplies the physical evaluation.

The architecture described above is the final design used to produce the results in Chapter 6, and differs from an earlier version in several respects motivated by training diagnostics (the reasoning behind each change, and the evidence that prompted it, is presented as Results in Section 6.6 rather than repeated here, since it is evidence, not method). The actor and critic share a single Graph Transformer encoder rather than each training an independent copy, so that both losses contribute gradient signal to the same graph representation instead of each network having to learn one from a much smaller effective sample. The entropy coefficient decays across training rather than remaining fixed, keeping exploration pressure higher earlier in a run when a structural action's probability is most at risk of collapsing on limited evidence. The fixed penalty applied to a colliding or infeasible child was reduced in magnitude so that it remains clearly the worst outcome without being an order of magnitude larger than any legitimate reward, and a floor is applied per action type to stop any single action's use from being driven all the way to zero. The objective-normalisation range used by the scalarised reward is also seeded from the initial Sobol-sampled population before any policy update, rather than being built up from an empty range during the first few generations. \missinginfo{Confirm the exact mechanism and parameters of the per-action-type floor/cap for an accurate description here.}

These changes prevent the reward or advantage signal for one action type from being computed relative to a pooled, cross-action-type baseline that a handful of early outcomes can dominate; they do not, on their own, make the advantage comparison genuinely fair \emph{between} action types. That distinction matters for interpreting the training behaviour reported in Section 6.6: the fixes stop total collapse to a single action, but a slower, partial drift toward the same class of low-risk actions can still occur, and is discussed there as a remaining limitation rather than a solved problem.

\section{NSGA-III Environmental Selection}
% [~1 page] MARKING: Understanding and Analysis (MED-HIGH); also directly
% supports O-4 ("performance-based metrics across generations", Section
% 1.3), which Chapter 6 Section 6.1/6.2 provides the evidence for.
% Reference-direction-based non-dominated sorting over the full
% 4-objective vector (no scalarization at selection time - contrast
% with the scalarized reward used for RL training in 4.5, and explain
% why these are deliberately different: NSGA-III explores the full
% Pareto front, while RL needs a single scalar training signal).
% Feasibility-first selection (constraint = simulation success/failure -
% NOT the structural constraints, which are enforced by construction;
% state this distinction explicitly, it directly supports your decision
% NOT to report a constraint-violation-rate metric - see Section 6.1).
% Explain reference-direction count and why it should scale with
% population size (das-Dennis partitions vs. population size) if this
% was addressed in your implementation.
NSGA-III ranks individuals using the complete four-objective vector rather than the scalarised value used for the PPO reward. All objectives are converted to a minimisation form for pymoo, while the attractive or repulsive configuration determines the direction of the two light-response objectives. Reference directions are used to retain solutions across the objective space. This is appropriate for four objectives because a single best score would hide useful trade-offs, while reference-direction niching helps maintain spread.

The number of reference directions is derived from the population size rather than fixed, so that niching resolution scales when the population is enlarged; a fixed reference-direction count tuned for a small population would otherwise under-provision diversity preservation once the population grows, since each direction would then have to represent proportionally more individuals. Selection also carries elitism: the best individuals found so far are retained explicitly across generations rather than being at risk of loss purely through the stochastic breeding and evaluation process, so that a strong candidate is not accidentally dropped from the population before its trade-offs have been fully explored by variation.

Feasibility is handled before objective comparison. Structural validity is enforced during graph construction, mirroring and grammar-masked variation, so illegal structural individuals do not need a separate violation score. Simulation failure or instability is treated separately as an infeasible evaluation. Thus, the collision gate handles representation-level feasibility, whereas the simulation constraint records whether a legal structure can also be evaluated successfully.

Hypervolume (HV) is the main many-objective convergence measure because it combines improvement and spread of the non-dominated set. GD, IGD and IGD+ were not used because this engineering problem has no known true Pareto front; constructing a best-known front from the same experiments would make the comparison partly circular. Best and mean scalarised fitness are included as a simpler view of population progress, but they are not used as the NSGA-III selection criterion.

The action distribution records how often each structural operator is chosen and can reveal collapse of mutation or crossover actions. Per-action reward and policy-loss plots help investigate why an action becomes common or rare. Finally, collision rate, defined as $n_{\mathrm{collided}}/n_{\mathrm{offspring}}$, indicates how many proposed offspring fail the geometric feasibility check. These metrics together describe optimisation progress and the behaviour of the operator policy.

The architectural changes described in Section 4.3 were motivated by an early observation, at small scale, that crossover actions (Graft Subtree, Swap Subtrees) collapsed to near-zero probability within roughly the first ten generations, in both pheromone-response arms independently. Ruling out the module-count budget as the cause (module counts stayed well under the structural limit throughout), the mechanism was identified as premature policy-entropy collapse compounded by a reward-scale mismatch: the fixed collision penalty was an order of magnitude larger than any legitimate reward delta, so a handful of early bad outcomes for one action type could dominate the shared advantage estimate before enough evidence accumulated to reassess it.

A longer, 200-generation run with the redesigned architecture (Section 4.3) was used to re-examine this behaviour. Collision avoidance improved substantially and consistently: across ten equal-sized blocks of the run, the collision rate fell from 43.2\% to 7.7\%, passing through 37.5\%, 31.9\%, 35.6\%, 31.4\%, 27.9\%, 21.7\%, 9.2\% and 13.7\% along the way. The decline is mostly monotonic and amounts to roughly an 80\% relative reduction, and is markedly stronger than the partial improvement visible at 40 generations (where the collision rate had only fallen to around 35\%) -- the longer run reveals a real trend that was not yet visible at the earlier checkpoint.

Reading the action-distribution data alongside this trend shows how the reduction was achieved. Structural actions that change graph topology, and therefore carry genuine collision risk -- Add Node, Prune Subtree, Delete Node, and the two crossover actions -- shrink from a substantial share of decisions early in the run to almost none by generation 150--200. In their place, Mutate Fold Type and Mutate Hinge Angle -- parameter adjustments to existing nodes that cannot trigger a geometric collision the way adding, deleting or reconnecting a module can -- grow to account for roughly 80--95\% of all decisions by the end of the run. This is the same underlying dynamic as the original small-scale collapse, occurring more slowly and only partially rather than immediately and completely: the policy is learning to avoid the collision penalty by avoiding the action types that risk it.

This picture is corroborated by the reward statistics. Restricting attention to non-collision transitions, the mean reward is essentially flat across the whole run (approximately $-0.05$ to $-0.08$ throughout, with no visible upward trend), while its standard deviation falls substantially, from around 0.20 to around 0.07. The policy is becoming more consistent, not more effective: it is converging on a narrower, safer distribution of actions without the average outcome of those actions improving. Taken together, the collision-rate, action-distribution and reward-variance evidence support a single interpretation: the policy has learned risk-avoidance, not design quality. The per-action-type floor introduced in Section 4.3 is doing the job it was intended for -- multiple action types remain in use, unlike the original run's complete collapse onto a single action -- but it does not, on its own, stop this slower drift toward the same class of low-risk actions.

This points to a specific, well-motivated next step rather than a general call for more training. The fixes applied so far prevent the collision penalty from being computed relative to a shared, cross-action-type baseline that a few outcomes can dominate; they do not make the advantage comparison itself fair \emph{between} action types with structurally different risk profiles. Genuine per-action-type reward or advantage normalisation -- comparing each action type's outcomes against its own typical range, rather than against a single pooled range -- was considered during this project but not implemented, and is proposed in Section 7.3 as the most likely route to a policy that values structural exploration when it pays off, rather than one that has only learned to penalise it when it does not.

The main limitation is scale. The documented configuration contains only five generations and one seed, so the convergence and RL-versus-random results are preliminary. The random-baseline result cannot establish a general advantage without longer runs and repeated seeds.

The light-response tests were performed on one simulated robot at a time. They do not demonstrate communication or coordination in a multi-robot swarm. The evolved morphologies were also not tested on physical Roblet hardware, so the conclusions are limited by the accuracy of the digital twin and its contact, folding and magnetic assumptions.

The RL diagnostics identified a remaining issue. At 200 generations, structural actions (Add Node, Prune Subtree, Delete Node, and the two crossover actions) shrink from a substantial share of decisions to near-zero by generation 150--200, while non-structural parameter actions (Mutate Fold Type, Mutate Hinge Angle) grow to account for roughly 80--95\% of decisions; over the same run the collision rate falls from 43.2\% to 7.7\% across ten deciles, but the non-collision reward's mean stays flat while its variance shrinks, indicating the policy has learned to avoid collision-risky actions rather than to design better morphologies with them. This is a slower, partial recurrence of the same failure mode the Section 4.3 fixes were designed to prevent, and is discussed in Section 6.6 and Section 7.3 rather than treated as resolved.
